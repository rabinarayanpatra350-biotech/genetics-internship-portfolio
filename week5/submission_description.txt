This Week 5 report is a critical review of genetic research methods and their ethical, legal and social implications, covering every objective of the task. It surveys the principal methodologies — sequencing from Sanger to long-read and single-cell, genome-wide association studies, family and linkage designs, biobanks, Mendelian randomisation, CRISPR screens and editing, somatic versus heritable gene therapy, polygenic scores, machine learning and multi-omics — and compares their advantages and limitations in a detailed table. It then argues that each method's limitation is frequently also its ethical problem: a polygenic score that does not travel across ancestries is both a scientific defect and a source of health inequity. A dedicated ethical section is organised around four domains — the individual, the family and community, society and the state, and the human future — and covers privacy and the myth of anonymisation, consent models and their limits, incidental findings and the right not to know, relational and community consent, insurance and employment discrimination, the ancestry equity gap, commercialisation, forensic genetic genealogy, heritable genome editing, and polygenic embryo screening. Seven documented cases are analysed in detail: Henrietta Lacks and HeLa (1951), the Havasupai tribe (1990–2010), Iceland's deCODE database, He Jiankui's gene-edited twins, forensic genetic genealogy, the first CRISPR sickle cell therapies, and India's National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission. Contested questions are presented with the strongest arguments on each side rather than one-sided advocacy. The review closes with a nine-step framework for integrating ethics into research workflows. All quantitative claims are attributed to verified sources, and the repository includes a fact-check ledger linking each key figure to its source.
