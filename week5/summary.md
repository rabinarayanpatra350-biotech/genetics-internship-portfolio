# Week 5 — Critical Review of Genetic Research Methods and Ethical Considerations

**Status:** submitted and graded | **Repository:** [genetics-week5-elsi-critical-review](https://github.com/rabinarayanpatra350-biotech/genetics-week5-elsi-critical-review) | **Release:** v1.0

## The task

Conduct a critical review of current genetic research methods while addressing the ethical, legal and social
implications (ELSI), including case studies, and propose how ethical practice can be integrated into research
workflows.

## What was produced

A **31-page critical review** (~10,000 words, 10 tables, 7 figures, **64 references**), a 279-word portal
description, a **fact-check ledger** mapping every quantitative claim to its source document, and a
reproducibility bundle.

## The argument

The review's organising claim is that **methodology and ethics are the same question asked in two
vocabularies**. A polygenic score that does not travel across ancestries is both a scientific defect and a
source of health inequity; the irreversibility of germline editing is both a technical property and the reason
it is governed differently from somatic therapy.

Three conclusions are stated at the outset:

1. The most serious harms in the field's history came from research that was **permitted and governed** — and
   then used in ways participants had not anticipated.
2. The populations least represented in research are systematically those for whom its results work least
   well, which makes diversity a scientific requirement, not only a matter of fairness.
3. Ethics confined to a paper's final section cannot change anything, because the consequential decisions are
   taken earlier.

## Coverage

**Methods compared** (11 in a detailed table): Sanger and next-generation sequencing, long-read and
single-cell methods, exome sequencing, GWAS, family and linkage studies, biobanks and cohorts, Mendelian
randomisation, CRISPR screens, somatic and heritable gene editing, base and prime editing, polygenic scores,
machine learning, liquid biopsy, multi-omics and synthetic biology.

**Ethics organised in four domains:** the individual (privacy and re-identification, consent, incidental
findings, the right not to know); the family and community (relational data, duty to warn, community consent
and Indigenous data sovereignty, benefit-sharing); society and the state (insurance and employment
discrimination, forensic genetic genealogy, commercialisation, equity, national screening programmes); and the
human future (heritable genome editing, embryo selection, enhancement, eugenic history).

## Seven documented case studies

| Case | Period |
|---|---|
| Henrietta Lacks and HeLa | 1951–2023 |
| The Havasupai tribe | 1990–2010 |
| Iceland and deCODE | 1998–2003 |
| He Jiankui's gene-edited twins | 2018–2019 |
| Forensic genetic genealogy | 2018– |
| First CRISPR sickle cell therapies | 2023 |
| India's National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission | 2023–2025 |

Plus two hypothetical scenarios — a polygenic score deployed without ancestry-specific calibration, and a
de-identified cohort with a re-identification route — chosen because neither requires bad intent, only
ordinary inertia.

## Contested questions presented as contested

Heritable genome editing, polygenic embryo screening, the disability-rights critique of prenatal screening and
the true prevalence of genetic discrimination are each presented with the strongest arguments on **both**
sides, with a note on what evidence would settle the disagreement. Where published estimates differ — 78%,
86.3% and 88.45% for the European-ancestry share of GWAS participants — the range is reported rather than the
most convenient figure, and the reason for the disagreement is explained.

## What it demonstrates

Literature synthesis at scale, balanced treatment of contested ethical questions, regulatory analysis across
international instruments and the Indian framework, and an evidence trail that maps every claim to its source.
